what abdurrahman ibn awf knew about money

the charity principle and the barakah effect · 9 min

most of us get our money advice from the wrong people. dave ramsey, robert kiyosaki, a tiktoker with a ring light. that is a shame, because we have our own teachers, and one of them ran circles around all of them fourteen hundred years ago.

abdurrahman ibn awf, may allah be pleased with him, was one of the ten companions promised paradise, and one of the wealthiest men of his time. he arrived in madinah with nothing, having left almost everything behind in makkah, and built it all back from the ground. by the end of his life the numbers are hard to even hold in your head. he died in 32 ah, and a single quarter of his estate was valued at eighty-four thousand dinars.

i want to focus on one thread of his life, because it is the one we understand least: how he gave, and what giving did to his wealth.

the ledger

first, the scale. these are not round numbers i am inventing. they come from the early biographers, tabaqat ibn sa'd and dhahabi's siyar.

700 camels
one caravan, loaded with goods, given away in a single afternoon when a hadith moved him.
50,000 dinars
in gold, given from his own wealth across his life.
1,500 camels
to equip an army in the path of allah, in one gift.
400 dinars each
left in his will to every surviving veteran of badr, plus a large legacy to the prophet's widows.
100 horses · 1,000 camels · 10,000 sheep
grazing on his land at al-baqi, at one point in his life.
84,000 dinars
the value of just one quarter of the estate he left behind.

he asked for the market, not a handout

before any of that, there was a man with nothing. when he reached madinah the prophet, peace be upon him, paired him with sa'd ibn ar-rabi', who offered him half of everything he owned. abdurrahman refused. "just show me the way to the market." he started trading dried yoghurt and butter, from zero, by choice. allah does not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves. self-reliance is not pride here. it is the default our deen sets.

and he sold with ihsan

he inspected his own goods. if something was flawed he marked it flawed and dropped the price rather than hide it. that is ihsan, excellence when no one is checking, and it built a reputation that made strangers comfortable buying from him. trust is the cheapest marketing there is and the most expensive thing to fake.

all of madinah lived off him

here is where it gets remarkable. talha, may allah be pleased with him, described the city's reliance on this one man in three parts: a third of madinah carried debts that abdurrahman paid off outright, a third took interest-free loans from him to start and grow their trade, and a third lived on his charity directly. "all of madinah," he said, "lives off abdurrahman."

the charity principle

now the part that sounds like poetry until you watch someone live it. the prophet, peace be upon him, said plainly:

"charity does not decrease wealth." (sahih muslim 2588)

read it once and it sounds like comfort for the giver. read it as abdurrahman did and it is a claim about how the world actually runs. the quran frames the same idea as a transaction with allah himself:

"who is it that will loan allah a goodly loan, so he may multiply it for him many times over?" (al-baqarah 2:245)

a loan, not a loss. and the word we use for the obligatory charity, zakat, carries it in its very root: the arabic z-k-w means both to purify and to grow. the same act cleans the wealth and increases it. that is not a metaphor stacked on top of the word. it is the word.

the barakah effect

so what is actually being promised? not a guaranteed return on investment. the word is barakah: blessing, an increase that runs past what the visible causes can explain. a small thing that stretches further than it should. an amount you gave away that finds its way back larger, through a door you were not watching.

abdurrahman lived inside that promise. the famous caravan, seven hundred camels long, was said to enter madinah while its tail had not yet left the far side of the city. when he heard the prophet, peace be upon him, warn about the burden of wealth in the next life, he wept and gave the entire caravan away on the spot, goods and all. to a spreadsheet that is ruin. what actually followed was more provision, again and again, until the numbers in the ledger above were possible. the money chased him precisely because he refused to be owned by it.

this is the hard reframe. hoarding feels like the safe way to grow, a number you guard and watch tick up. giving feels like the number going down. but the promise inverts it: the guarded pile is what stagnates, and the open hand is what compounds, because a different kind of accounting takes over.

he kept the books on his soul

and still he was afraid of it. a lavish meal was once set in front of him and he wept, remembering a companion who died with almost nothing. "i fear we have been given our reward in this life instead of the next." a man already promised paradise, still auditing his own heart. narrations describe him literally tallying it: how many camels, how much gold, set against how much had gone out in the path of allah. let every soul look to what it has sent ahead for tomorrow. he made gratitude an action and then he checked the balance.

none of it was a trade-off

that is the thing that gets me. he did not get rich despite his deen, or stay poor because of it. the wealth and the worship were a single motion. the giving was not a tax on the building; it was the engine of it. you can build something large and keep your heart in the next life. you just have to hold it open-handed, the way he did.

so before i take money advice from a stranger online, i want to sit with men like this first. that is the wealth i actually want: load-bearing for the people around me, and weightless on my own heart. inshallah.

sources: tabaqat ibn sa'd; dhahabi, siyar a'lam an-nubala; sahih muslim 2588; sahih al-bukhari; qur'an 2:245, 13:11, 14:7. i first read a fuller telling of his life on islamicfinanceguru.
