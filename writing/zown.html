Preface

zown is a digital real estate platform in canada aimed at making home buying more accessible by returning part of the commission to buyers.

Overview

i spent my time at zown as the fifth engineer, working on zoro (the ai realtor), an api that serves home data, and hubspot.

they say you can never find the same person twice (even in the same person). that's how i felt after my stint at zown, and how i feel writing this now, heading back to school.

first impressions

on my first day, i didn't really know what to expect. i had been around friends who worked full time around the world, who told me about the culture they dealt with and experience. however, in my mind, i had one goal, result oriented engineering. i knew from past experiences that i didn't want to get caught in the "barbwire" of busywork, so i immediately started asking about bottlenecks and tried identifying which giant i could take down. my role was never concretely defined by an outcome, however, the accountability was. in the first week, i had already started debating and questioning the way the processes were being used right now. obviously, i don't know everything, but it is imperative that i assume i can do anything. that led to some fun and interesting arguments i took part in.

out of the several problems and decisions i owned in my time at zown, there are three big ones that i'll be covering in this piece.

focus 1: zoro

The most interesting problem I got to work on at Zown started with Ibrahim Awad and a question: how would an AI realtor actually work? Buying a home is one of the biggest decisions someone makes, so handing the whole thing to an agent felt wrong. Instead we took a realtor’s tasks one at a time: the CMA, response times, follow-ups, listing alerts, the questions that came in at odd hours that our realtors couldn’t answer. But we knew there always has to be a human in the loop, and in this case, a realtor in the loop.

from a sales tool to something that had to ship

This project didn’t start as a consumer chatbot. It started as an internal sales tool, so account managers’ chats were taken care of while they were away. Most of this happened in WhatsApp groups, where realtors were answering chats and updating HubSpot by hand. At first we had Zoro announce himself: “I’m [agent name]’s assistant!”, even though the messages came from the same number as the realtor.

We built the agent in Python + LangChain first. After the first version, I went AM by AM (account manager by account manager), and we deployed it to 12 chats and collected feedback on performance, effectiveness, and style. Then we rewrote it in TypeScript on Mastra. The LangChain version leaned on a lot of quality checks and confidence scoring, because we were asking the model to come up with the answer itself, and then had to check whether it was right. In the rewrite I cut most of those extra checks. Instead of asking the model to generate a solution, we designed tools for it: pull the listing, run the CMA, look up the buyer’s stage. The model picks the right tool, and the tool gives a correct answer. The shape stayed simple: check the message coming in, let the model think and use tools, check the reply going out. The old Python file names still sit in the comments (Migrates: backend/agents/orchestrator.py), because a rewrite is a conversation with the last version of yourself.

We created a dev environment with demo listings instead of the full GTA database, which was constantly updating, and set up a test WhatsApp number. Then we put the bot in a container on Fargate. Its files (the knowledge base, CMA PDFs) sit in storage, and follow-ups, reminders and listing alerts run on cron jobs. The old Python service stayed up on EC2 beside the new one for a long time. Moving WhatsApp over was one setting in Maytapi, the service that connects us to WhatsApp, so moving back was the same setting.

After switching over, we noticed a significant downgrade in the quality of conversations.

Then we gave it tools to read listing data, which was harder than it sounds. The Toronto real estate boards don’t hand you a neat library. TRREB and ITSO send listings as data feeds, photos come separately, and a listing without a published photo isn’t one you can show. Repliers gave us the live MLS. We needed it, but it wasn’t enough on its own.

He had a name change along the way: Zara, then Zoro. Openly AI, all lowercase, no “as a language model.” The voice was the easy part. Knowing what he was allowed to do was not.

Some of the last things I did at Zown lived on the phone: designing the mobile chat, tuning the vibration as messages came in until it felt right, and fixing the streaming (SSE) issues between the agent and the app.

having the listing was one thing

One of the hardest problems was the logic. Having the listing data was one thing. Knowing what to do with it was another.

In the market we worked in, sellers played one of two games. Some listed a home way too cheap, then came back with “I have five offers” to push the price up. Others listed at what they wanted and waited.

Zoro needed to tell those apart, and the price on its own wasn’t enough.

We never wrote a playbook for how sellers should list. We wrote the buyer’s side, because that’s the side Zoro plays. In a hot market, the listing agent holds every offer until a set date instead of reviewing them as they arrive. The list price is bait, and the home usually sells over asking. “Offers welcome anytime” means the opposite. And a false warning is worse than saying nothing.

So Zoro needed more than listPrice. We read the listing’s public remarks for an offer date, and pulled comparable sales to see whether similar homes went for $100k or more above the ask. When both showed up together, we didn’t leave it to the model’s judgement. Zoro added a fixed heads-up in his own voice and flagged a human. The price was a hint. The combination was the fact.

That’s the pattern that kept coming back: don’t ask the language model to make up policy. Give it the extra inputs, then hard-code the dangerous sentence.

stage is a permission, not a vibe

Rishard’s point was the same idea, one level up. A buyer who never filled out the qualification form isn’t the same as one who filled it out and scored low. Someone with a deal already has an agent. Someone under contract shouldn’t be pitched a new search. Someone on hold should be nurtured, not treated like a fresh lead.

Zown already tracked all of this. The mobile app used qualification status to decide who could book showings, and HubSpot had a deal pipeline. Our first AI analysis looked at the wrong field, one that in practice only ever moved from contact to lead, and concluded the later stages never happened. It was wrong. The real stage lived on the deal. We copied its name into our own database exactly as HubSpot had it, rather than mapping it to a fixed list that would break the first time someone renamed a stage.

So every buyer was described by two separate facts: their form score, and where they were in HubSpot. Together those made seven buyer states. Where they were in HubSpot beat the score, because a deal means an agent no matter what the form said. A stage name we didn’t recognise still meant “they have an agent.” Guessing the other way, and treating someone on WhatsApp as a stranger who never qualified, was exactly the mistake we’d been told not to make.

The rules became a table: eleven actions by seven states, and the build fails if any cell is empty. Omar Awad reviewed it, and not all of our defaults survived. We’d treated a low score the same as a missing form. He reversed that. Completing the form is the gate. Passing it is not. That change mattered outside the bot too: more people could book showings in the app, and showings cost real time. It deserved a product decision, not a quiet ride-along on an AI ticket.

The delicate part was money. One review said: never imply the buyer isn’t qualified. Another said: point out when lenders probably won’t approve this purchase. Those only clash if you treat a person and a price as the same thing, so that’s where we drew the line. No tier names, no “you don’t qualify,” no internal labels like MQB out loud. Saying what a lender is likely to think about a specific price is a different sentence, and sometimes a required one.

Four hard limits sat above the table. Zoro never submits, counters, or negotiates an offer. Once a deal is firm, he points the buyer to their account manager. Internal language never reaches a client. And he never passes judgement on the buyer.

We didn’t trust a prompt to remember any of this, so we enforced it in code. A check on incoming messages catches the never-allowed cases. The prompt only shows the rules that apply to this buyer right now. A second check reviews every reply before it goes out, and the model can’t talk its way past it. Even the HubSpot stages Zoro was allowed to change, he could only propose. We were never told how long buyers should sit in each stage, so we didn’t guess. Failing closed is what compliance looks like when you haven’t been told the rule.

Cadence (asking every three months, not every chat) we wrote down but never fully wired up. That’s part of the story too. Connecting AI to existing rules is a lot of plumbing, and some of the pipes are still capped.

focus 2: api

Underneath everything was the data: an API that serves home data. Live search and filters, photos, property history, analytics, price estimates, alerts, and webhooks.

We were using Repliers, but it couldn’t give us everything we needed. So we started building our own API.

Repliers stayed as the live MLS. In a conversation fresh data matters, so sold history and photo search still go there. Everything the buyer sees in the app (the map, the filters, the photos) moved to us: board feeds come in, get cleaned up, and land in Postgres. The listings API reads only from that database and never touches Repliers. On the agent side, every MLS request goes through one connector, so switching providers one day means changing one file. When Zoro searches, he asks Repliers first and tops up from our database if results are thin. The old order is still one setting away, because a switch you can’t undo isn’t a switch.

I used Apple Maps to understand what’s around each home. Every address gets pinned on the map when it comes in, and a sweep with OpenStreetMap fills a catalog of nearby places. When a listing page asks what’s nearby, we don’t call Apple again. We ask our database (PostGIS) what’s within a kilometre of a point we already stored.

For schools, I went looking for test scores: Ontario school lists, EQAO results, Fraser rankings, catchment maps. We used to lean on a third party to tell us what a neighbourhood was like. We replaced that with our own data: census, climate, walk and transit scores, market stats, schools. It refreshes on a schedule, with a lock so two jobs never scrape the province twice.

Zoro doesn’t read that whole pile. He makes one lookup against a summary table (a materialized view): the home and everything around it, already joined, in a shape he can say out loud. That was the point of all the plumbing. Zoro could only know what he was talking about if the answer arrived pre-chewed.

Price estimates split the same way. The listing page gets an automated estimate from the API. In chat, Zoro builds a CMA from live comparable sales plus our own math. Alerts come from saved searches and from price changes we actually saw come in. History is a tool Zoro can call, not every MLS record dumped into the prompt.

A lot of local data, one door for the brain. That’s the unglamorous half of “we gave it tools to connect to the real estate board.”

focus 3: hubspot

I think the first time I got access to HubSpot at Zown, I almost shed a tear. Not because it was beautiful.

For Zoro to know where a customer stood, the data had to be clean enough to trust. It wasn’t. Every record had 700+ properties and was tangled in multiple workflows. The advice I got was to go through it by hand.

Dr. Awad and I took a bomboclaude of agents and went through the processes and properties one by one.

The most enjoyable part was not unweeding the CRM. It was introducing Omar to Claude and Claude Code, and watching him fall in love with using MCPs in real time. Back in April, we were using a special app to go through and manage HubSpot.

What we didn’t do after that was copy the mess into our own database. HubSpot stayed the source of truth for sales. We wrote a list of approved fields, so the website form and the mobile app send exactly the same data. Some forms called the same field cid and others zcid, so we treated them as one, because HubSpot will happily reject you for a duplicate. Outgoing updates wait in a queue, so a flaky request can’t create two leads. Each buyer gets one qualification lead, and account managers own the pipeline after that. Editing a form gets a different note title from submitting it, so a workflow that calls people on “Form Submission” doesn’t ring someone again because they fixed a typo.

Coming the other way, we listened for deal stage changes and saved the stage name exactly as written. We ignored the echo when HubSpot told us about a deal we’d just created ourselves. And we never let the chat app set a stage. If you could send HIGHLY_QUALIFIED from the chat, you could talk the bot past every rule in the table.

Admin CRM, our ops tool, doesn’t talk to HubSpot at all. Two systems, side by side, on purpose. Keeping them in sync was a problem we chose not to have.

The unweeding was miserable. The part I would do again is sitting with Omar while the tools clicked. The playbook he later reviewed is the same person, looking at the same customer, from the other side of the wall: what may the bot do, right now, for this buyer.
