my first year at waterloo

october 22nd, 2026 · 9 min

before waterloo

My experience at Waterloo started three months before my first day. Back then, I was absolutely convinced that I would never step foot on a university campus. I was running a social media marketing agency in downtown Toronto, cold calling random realtors and pitching them a package where I'd make them a website, automate everything they had using n8n and GoHighLevel, and film 5 videos for them a week. I was sure that this was my life, and this was the exceptional life that I could live after scaling this agency. Then, after a cold splash of reality, I lost all my clients. I started doubting myself, my potential, my purpose, and any/all sense of direction.

I started waking up in cold sweats, waking up daily for tahajjud, praying isthikhara, and begging Allah SWT to pull me off my face. I remember locking in for studies, even though I'd been laughed at for even thinking about Waterloo, but in my heart, I refused to be told no. I made du'a and put my best foot forward. I knew if I wanted change it wasn't going to come from staying in the same environment I was in, so I stopped trying the agency route and vowed to give my academics one last shot. I started sitting at the Masjid every day after fajr for hours on end, tuning myself as much as I could with extra nafl, classes, and isthikhara. Alhamdulillah, in May, I got my acceptance letters from the University. I still remember breaking the news to my parents. My father was in Karachi, Pakistan, in the midst of another conflict between India & Pakistan. He was showing us a missile leaving Karachi and slowly flying up in the distance when all of a sudden I blurted out,

"baba i got into waterloo"

After a minute of empty silence, confused stares, and me grinning, he asked, "are you joking?"

Having been homeschooled my whole life, my parents weren't really ready to let me go like that, nor did they really think I was going to pull it off. I've always been apart from your average student or hobbyist. I always looked for the best way to express myself creatively, and that's my bar of success: not money, scale, or status, but fulfillment through expression.

Having this option in my hands felt weird. I'd just seen one of my friends buy an AMG in cash, another just got married, and another was leaving for Pakistan to fast-track medicine. I've never really felt an attraction to a certain path, but I knew this was the answer to my isthikhara. I had a purpose. I had something to fulfill here, and that's why Allah SWT was pulling me there.

first semester

As I packed all my belongings into three black and green heavy-duty boxes and threw all my extra stuff away, I called Arfeen Zaffar, one of my closest friends & one of the reasons I knew about Waterloo in the first place. He told me I only had to survive the first year financially; that would be the most suffocating part, but after the first co-op I would feel freed. Most people advised me to just take OSAP, but a bug in me said no. I didn't really want to, so I took on three jobs, and on my first day of school, I skipped orientation to work. I was hard stuck on paying for the next semester as fast as I could, and then figuring out which direction I wanted to take my life in.

I noticed a couple of things about the culture at Waterloo. The first was that everyone seemed to be in a hurry, somewhere to be, something more important to do. I noticed that people ranked conversations by who you were, rather than how you were. It honestly felt like I had just thrown myself into the hunger games, where everyone was competing at how fast they could reach the top. Don't get me wrong, it does have its benefits, but my desire for community was first satiated when I went for Fajr at the musallah at Waterloo. There I met some of the humblest brothers I had ever met: kind, caring, focused, and driven. Something I noticed about this group was that they too struggled with the feeling of not having done enough, but rather than sulking over that, they were in the moment. As the days went on I met others who were similar, not Muslim, but kind, helpful, and humble. I knew that was who I wanted to surround myself with. I usually found these people in their element at coffee shops, events, and hackathons.

goosetype

While I was falling in love with the environment, I was working 1am shifts, skipping meals, and slowly watching myself burn out. After one of the hackathons I attended, I met a YC startup founder who offered me an internship interview: if I could get something used 1000 times on the internet, he would give me an interview. That left a flame in my heart. I had worked on systems that got users before, but never a product of my own. That night, I stayed up building a silly little imitation of my favorite game, monkeytype, and I called it WaterlooType. I checked if people were Waterloo students by sending them an email asking, "are you a waterloo student?" Looking back, it doesn't feel like a bright decision, and as a result, within the first hour and 50 users later, I got banned from sending emails to Waterloo students. I posted about it online and saw there was demand from other universities, so I refunded the domain and bought goosetype.com. I also removed signup and implemented a small algorithm to make sure spam & malicious entries were denied.

Alhamdulillah, within a week I had 10,000 tests taken.

I rushed back to the founder with messages and screenshots, but to my dismay I was told there was no longer any position to join. I wondered how many other people this had happened to. I knew I wasn't going to let this be my reality any longer, so I cold emailed my way into my first tiny stealth startup & quit the restaurant job & cleaning job I was working at the time. I then joined a growth fellowship, VC scout positions, and another part-time software contract that I found.

ummahhacks

Around this time, my heart started feeling really strongly about creating my own sort of hackathon, something that solved the problems I'd seen first hand. I was taking a fiqh course at the time, and all I could think about was how there seems to be a huge disconnect between the average student at a hackathon and the intentionality and beauty of the lifestyle we live as Muslims. I remember talking to Ibrahim Ansari, and that was the first conversation I ever had about UmmahHacks. I quickly pitched the idea to Afifa Siddiqua, and we decided we would schedule it two months down the line in January, right before Ramadan. I think Afifa & Ibrahim can attest to the insane amount of work we put in to make this a reality. It would not have been possible without them.

I remember cold DMing, emailing, calling, and talking to anybody/everybody willing to listen. In one of the early calls, someone told us this idea was pointless and we would only be able to get a total of 50 people.

I remember vividly wondering, is this worth the time I'm throwing at it? Looking back, there was something more gnawing at me: knowing that if we could help at least one kid see the world more intentionally, it would be worth it.

I remember telling my friends and family, and as they watched me pour my time into this, they thought I was insane. This wasn't something average at Waterloo, nor was it a mission with a blueprint, but something in me wanted to pull this off so bad, and thanks to Afifa and Ibrahim we pushed through.

san francisco

In late December I flew to Pakistan to visit my family, around when we closed some of our first main sponsors. None of my family really understood what I was doing or where I was going, but they saw how passionate I was and let me do my thing. While I was there, I was convincing my Dad to let me fly to SF. I had never been there, but I did have an interview. At first I was met with a hard no, but a couple of isthikharas and some convincing later, I was met with a yes.

I took the last chunk of savings I had and flew to SF. Thanks to one of my really close friends, Ibrahim Nabi, I got a discount on the flight.

I landed in early January. It was a chilly night, and I hopped straight onto the Caltrain, which I took straight to one of Safi Qadir's events; he was hosting a Halaqah in San Francisco. Safi took care of me like an older brother. He drove me around, took me out for food, and showed me hospitality I'll never forget. I met amazing people like Abir, Mustafa, and many, many more. I stayed at Yasin's Powell House and met a bunch of other youth with burning dreams and desires.

On this trip, I spent a lot of time with Shayaan Azeem, who I can't thank enough for his advice, love, and care. He introduced me to his friends, and we went around for food and sightseeing.

During this whole period, I kept pitching UmmahHacks to as many people as I could. I told people I was sitting on the oil of Canada: resumes from Waterloo, UofT, and some of the most insane resumes they'd ever seen. This worked, and I was able to secure a substantial amount in sponsorships that day.

the weekend

The week leading up to UmmahHacks, the three of us held calls daily, almost hourly, making sure logistics would go smoothly, with random tasks constantly popping up: paying vendors, dealing with flops, and different issues that kept coming up. Three days before the event, we realized Canada would be having one of the most insane snowstorms we had ever seen. On Friday, we went in person to pick up our first group of speakers, none of whom, surprisingly, had been able to secure a hotel. So I did the logical thing: I set up sleeping bags and air mattresses in my studio near campus, and subhanallah, the barakah in that room was like no other. We had 8 people in that room, people I looked up to, people I am glad to call friends, sleeping in bags across from my air fryer.

Around 1 AM, Afifa called me: "muhib, we don't have a schedule for tomorrow." I calmly walked outside the apartment, and in the hall, right then and there, we came up with the whole itinerary for the weekend.

Alhamdulillah, looking back at the event, I think everything that could have gone wrong went wrong, yet it was a better outcome than I could have ever asked or prayed for. As I look at the archives of the event, I am grateful, and ever so determined to make the next UmmahHacks ever so much greener. The goal for this year is so much bigger, not in the SF way, but in the Waterloo way. The goal is simply to build an environment where your deen and your dunya can genuinely grow together. If we can shift just one person's perspective, if we can inspire just one builder to see that their work serves a higher purpose, then every late night, every cold email, and every moment of doubt will be completely worth it.

If this resonated with you and you want to help us organize the next UmmahHacks, my inbox is always open. We're always looking for builders who care about intention over scale.
