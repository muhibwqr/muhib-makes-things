faith & wealth

    
what abdurrahman ibn awf knew about money

    

      by muhib waqar · 9 min read
    

     the barakah loop, interactive →
    

      
    

  


  


    
i want to build something huge. i also believe you only get exactly what is written for you, and that i'll answer for every dirham of it one day. those two things pull at each other, and most of the money advice online does not even know the tension exists. dave ramsey, kiyosaki, a tiktoker with a ring light. so i stopped taking it from strangers and went looking for someone who actually lived both at once. not an influencer. a sahabi.


    
the gist: abdurrahman ibn awf arrived in madinah with nothing and died one of the richest men of his era, giving away fortunes the whole way. the part that stops me is that the giving was not a tax on the building. it was the engine. that is the model i want: build big, hold it loose, and let barakah do the compounding.


    
abdurrahman ibn awf, may allah be pleased with him, was one of the ten promised paradise. he left almost everything in makkah, rebuilt it from zero, and by the end the numbers are hard to even hold. a single quarter of his estate was worth eighty-four thousand dinars. i have started things from nothing and lost more than i have made, so "rebuilt it from zero" is the line that hooked me.


    
the ledger

    
the scale of it

    
first the scale, because i did not believe it either. these are not numbers i am rounding up for effect. they come from the early biographers, tabaqat ibn sa'd and dhahabi's siyar a'lam an-nubala.


    

      
700 camels
one caravan of goods, given away in a single afternoon when a hadith moved him.

      
50,000 dinars
in gold, given from his own wealth across his life.

      
1,500 camels
to equip an army in the path of allah, in one gift.

      
400 dinars each
left in his will to every surviving veteran of badr, plus a legacy to the prophet's widows.

      
100 horses · 1,000 camels · 10,000 sheep
grazing on his land at al-baqi at one point in his life.

      
84,000 dinars
the value of just one quarter of the estate he left behind.

    


    
where it began

    
he asked for the market, not a handout

    
before any of that, there was a man with nothing, which is the part i can actually relate to. the prophet, peace be upon him, paired him with sa'd ibn ar-rabi', who offered him half of everything he owned. abdurrahman refused. "just show me the way to the market." being homeschooled burned one thing into me: you do not wait for permission, you go figure it out. that is the same instinct. he did not want the handout, he wanted the market. and he sold with ihsan: he marked his own flawed goods and dropped the price instead of hiding the flaw. i have watched people cut exactly that corner to win one sale. he understood trust compounds longer than any single deal does.


    
the reach

    
all of madinah lived off him

    
this is where it stops being a hustle story and turns into something i actually want. talha, may allah be pleased with him, described the city's reliance on this one man in three parts: a third of madinah carried debts that abdurrahman paid off outright, a third took interest-free loans from him to start and grow their trade, and a third lived on his charity directly. "all of madinah," he said, "lives off abdurrahman." imagine your wealth being load-bearing for a whole city. that is the kind of rich worth chasing.


    

    
the charity principle

    
a loan, not a loss

    
here is the line that used to sound like a nice quote to me, until i started taking it seriously as someone who actually wants to build wealth. the prophet, peace be upon him, said plainly:


    

      
"charity does not decrease wealth."

      
sahih muslim 2588

    


    
read it once and it sounds like comfort for the giver. read it the way abdurrahman did and it becomes a claim about how the world actually runs. i am ambitious enough to want to hear "charity does not decrease wealth" as a strategy, not just a consolation. the quran frames the same idea as a transaction with allah himself: "who is it that will loan allah a goodly loan, so he may multiply it for him many times over?" (al-baqarah 2:245). a loan, not a loss. for anyone trying to build, that reframes the whole thing.

    
and the word we use for obligatory charity, zakat, carries it in its very root. the arabic z-k-w means both to purify and to grow. the same act cleans the wealth and increases it. that is not a metaphor stacked on top of the word. it is the word.


    
the barakah effect

    
giving is subtraction for one step

    
so what is actually being promised? not an roi i can put in a model. the word is barakah: blessing, an increase that runs past what the visible causes can explain. a small thing that stretches further than it should. an amount you gave away that finds its way back larger, through a door you were not watching.

    
abdurrahman lived inside that promise. the famous caravan, seven hundred camels long, was said to be entering madinah while its tail had not yet left the far side of the city. when he heard the prophet, peace be upon him, warn about the burden of wealth in the next life, he wept and gave the entire caravan away on the spot, goods and all. could i give away my best year in a single afternoon because a hadith landed on me? not yet, honestly. but that is the bar he set, and i would rather aim at it than pretend it is not there. to a spreadsheet that move is ruin. what actually followed was more provision, again and again, until the numbers above were possible. the money chased him precisely because he refused to be owned by it.


    

    
this is the hard reframe, especially for someone like me who likes watching a number go up. hoarding feels like the safe way to grow. giving feels like the number going down. but the promise inverts it: the guarded pile is what stagnates, and the open hand is what compounds, because a different kind of accounting takes over.


    

    
and still he feared it

    
he kept the books on his soul

    
a lavish meal was once set in front of him and he wept, remembering a companion who died with almost nothing. at seventeen i ran an agency partly so i could ride around in realtors' corvettes and lambos, chasing exactly that kind of meal. "i fear we have been given our reward in this life instead of the next." a man already promised paradise, still auditing his own heart. narrations describe him literally tallying it: how many camels, how much gold, set against how much had gone out in the path of allah. let every soul look to what it has sent ahead for tomorrow. he made gratitude an action and then he checked the balance.


    
the takeaway

    
none of it was a trade-off

    
that is the thing that gets me. he did not get rich despite his deen, or stay small because of it. the wealth and the worship were a single motion. that is the founder i am trying to become: building beautiful things, load-bearing for the people around me, and weightless on my own heart. i am working to be remembered, not known. inshallah.


    
sources: tabaqat ibn sa'd; dhahabi, siyar a'lam an-nubala; sahih muslim 2588; sahih al-bukhari; qur'an 2:245, 13:11, 14:7. i first read a fuller telling of his life on islamicfinanceguru.
